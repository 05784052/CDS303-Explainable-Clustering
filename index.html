CDS303-Explainable-Clustering

Explainable Clustering of Seoul Commercial Districts Based on Transaction Volume and Average Transaction Value

Course: CDS 303 – Scientific Data Mining
Team: G9
Team Members: Hyukjoon Choi and Yujin Yang

Project Overview

This project aims to identify and explain distinct types of commercial districts in Seoul based on transaction volume and average transaction value. Using estimated sales data from 2025, we will examine differences in commercial activity and identify groups of districts with similar transaction characteristics.

Our analysis focuses on two aspects of commercial activity: how many transactions occur within a commercial district and how much is spent per transaction on average. These measures may reveal different commercial activity profiles, such as districts characterized by high transaction volumes and relatively low average transaction values or districts with fewer transactions but higher average transaction values.

We will use an explainable clustering framework to identify these profiles and investigate the characteristics that distinguish them. The findings may provide useful insights for small-business support organizations and commercial district policy planners seeking to understand differences in commercial activity across Seoul.

Research Question

What distinct types of Seoul's commercial districts can be identified based on transaction volume and average transaction value, and which features best explain these differences?

Dataset

Dataset: Seoul Commercial District Analysis Service — Estimated Sales by Commercial District (2025)

Source: 서울특별시_우리마을가게 상권분석서비스(상권-추정매출)

The dataset contains 85,732 records and 55 variables covering the four quarters of 2025. It includes estimated sales amounts, transaction counts, commercial district identifiers, service industry categories, and sales breakdowns by day of the week, time period, gender, and customer age group.

The original data is organized by year-quarter, commercial district, and service industry. For this project, we will aggregate service industry records within each commercial district and quarter.

Unit of Analysis and Key Variables

Unit of analysis: Commercial district × quarter
Study period: 2025, Q1–Q4

Each observation will represent one commercial district in a particular quarter. This approach allows us to examine quarterly differences in commercial activity. A district may therefore belong to different clusters across quarters.

We will focus on two clustering features:

  1. Transaction volume: The total estimated number of transactions within a commercial district during a quarter. We plan to apply a log(1 + x) transformation to reduce the influence of extremely large transaction counts.
  2. Average transaction value: Total estimated sales divided by total estimated transaction counts for each district–quarter observation.

Both features will be standardized before clustering. Observations with zero transactions will be examined separately because their average transaction value cannot be calculated.

The log transformation will reduce differences in scale but will not eliminate the influence of district size. Accordingly, the analysis will characterize commercial activity patterns that may also reflect differences in the scale of commercial districts.

Proposed Analytical Framework

We will follow the four-stage explainable clustering framework introduced in the assigned research papers:

  1. Data preprocessing: Aggregate the original records, calculate average transaction value, examine data quality, and transform and standardize the selected features.
  2. Dimensionality reduction: Evaluate whether dimensionality reduction is appropriate given the two-feature clustering design. We will avoid reducing dimensions solely to apply a technique if doing so would unnecessarily discard useful information.
  3. Clustering: Identify groups of district–quarter observations based on transaction volume and average transaction value. The number of clusters will be selected through evaluation rather than predetermined.
  4. Classification for explainability: Use the generated cluster labels as classification targets to identify the features that distinguish the resulting groups.

We may also examine service industry composition and sales patterns by time of day, day of the week, and customer age group to provide additional context for interpreting the clusters. These characteristics are not part of the initial two-feature clustering design.

Expected Contribution

The project seeks to develop interpretable profiles of commercial districts based on their transaction characteristics. Rather than assuming a fixed set of district types, we will determine the number and characteristics of clusters through analysis.

The resulting profiles may help describe how commercial activity differs across Seoul's districts and quarters. Any interpretations will be based on the observed data and evaluated clustering results rather than predefined categories.

Paper Summary

Paper 1: A Comprehensive Framework for Explainable Cluster Analysis

Authors: Miguel Alvarez-Garcia, Raquel Ibar-Alonso, and Mar Arenas-Parra (2024)
Journal: Information Sciences , 663, 120282
DOI: https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ins.2024.120282

This paper introduces a four-step framework for explainable cluster analysis. The authors emphasize that clustering results should be both reliable and interpretable. Because clustering is an unsupervised learning method, it can identify groups of similar observations without directly explaining which characteristics distinguish those groups. The proposed framework addresses this challenge by combining data preparation, dimensionality reduction, clustering, and classification.

The Four-Step Framework

1. Data Preprocessing

The first step prepares the dataset for analysis by addressing data quality issues and transforming variables into suitable formats. This is important because missing values, inconsistent measurements, and differences in variable scales can affect clustering results.

2. Dimensionality Reduction

The second step reduces the complexity of the dataset while attempting to preserve important information. This can help address redundancy and make high-dimensional data more manageable for clustering. The usefulness of dimensionality reduction depends on the characteristics of the dataset.

3. Clustering

The third step groups observations according to their similarities. Unlike supervised classification, clustering does not require predefined target labels. Its purpose is to discover patterns and identify meaningful groups within the data.

4. Classification for Explainability

The final step uses the cluster labels generated during clustering as target labels for a supervised classification model. The classifier learns to distinguish the identified groups using the available features. Its predictions and feature contributions can then help explain the characteristics associated with each cluster.

Classification does not create the original clusters. Instead, it provides a way to interpret and characterize the groups discovered through unsupervised learning.

Main Takeaway

The paper demonstrates that identifying clusters is only part of a useful analysis. Understanding the features that distinguish those clusters is also essential for interpreting the results and communicating their practical significance.

Paper 2: Uncovering Student Profiles: An Explainable Cluster Analysis Approach to PISA 2022

Authors: Miguel Alvarez-Garcia, Mar Arenas-Parra, and Raquel Ibar-Alonso (2024)
Journal: Computers & Education , 223, 105166
DOI: https://doi.org/10.1016/j.compedu.2024.105166

This paper applies the explainable clustering framework to PISA 2022 student data. The researchers use the methodology to identify student profiles and investigate the characteristics that distinguish them.

The study identifies seven student clusters and uses supervised classification to interpret the resulting profiles. An XGBoost classifier achieves an overall accuracy of 0.8643 in predicting cluster assignments. The researchers also use SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations) values to examine how different features contribute to the model's predictions.

SHAP helps explain both overall patterns in the classification model and the factors influencing individual predictions. By combining clustering, classification, and model interpretation, the study moves beyond identifying student groups to describing the characteristics associated with those groups.

Main Takeaway

This application illustrates how an explainable clustering framework can transform complex data into interpretable profiles. It also demonstrates why classification is useful after clustering: the classifier provides an additional method for examining the features that distinguish the identified groups.

Application to Our Project

We will apply the same four-stage framework to estimated sales data from Seoul's commercial districts in 2025. Our unit of analysis will be the commercial district–quarter observation, and our two initial clustering features will be transaction volume and average transaction value.

1. Data Preprocessing

We will aggregate sales amounts and transaction counts across service industries for each commercial district and quarter. Average transaction value will be calculated by dividing total estimated sales by total estimated transaction counts. We will examine missing values, duplicate records, zero transaction counts, and outliers. Transaction volume will undergo a log(1 + x) transformation, and both clustering features will be standardized.

2. Dimensionality Reduction

We will evaluate whether Principal Component Analysis (PCA) is appropriate for our two-feature design. Because our initial dataset for clustering contains only two features, dimensionality reduction may provide limited benefits and could remove information needed to distinguish transaction volume from average transaction value. We will assess its usefulness rather than assume that reducing the number of dimensions is necessary.

3. Clustering

We will group commercial district–quarter observations according to transaction volume and average transaction value. The number of clusters will be determined through evaluation rather than fixed in advance. Potential profiles may differ in their combinations of transaction volume and average transaction value, but the actual cluster characteristics will be determined by the analysis.

4. Classification for Explainability

After generating cluster labels, we will use them as the target variable in a supervised classification model. We will examine which features help distinguish the resulting clusters and consider additional characteristics, such as commercial district type, industry composition, and sales patterns, when interpreting the groups.

If the classification model uses the same two features as the clustering algorithm, its results will characterize the existing cluster structure rather than independently validate it. We will interpret the findings accordingly.

Conclusion

The two papers provide both a methodological framework and a practical example of explainable cluster analysis. Our project will use these ideas to identify different types of commercial activity in Seoul and explain the characteristics associated with the resulting clusters. The objective is not only to group commercial districts but also to make the identified patterns understandable and useful for interpretation.
